# Character

Give the author a way to decide what this person will do or say in the next scene. Use the author's premise, manuscript excerpts, and relevant notes to identify established facts, genre expectations, narrative register, and point of view. Treat new ideas as proposals until the author accepts them. A few lines of input are enough to begin. State an assumption if it materially shapes the result. Ask a focused question if guessing would change the character's role or conflict with an established decision.

## Create from a premise

- Give one working interpretation in a sentence. Describe a recognizable pattern of behavior and explain what it brings to this story. Offer a second interpretation when it leads to a substantially different choice in the same situation.
- Work out what the character wants in the immediate scene, what they risk to get it, and what habit takes over under pressure. Show how that habit changes with someone close to them or with someone who has power over them. Put any sample dialogue in a situation where the character wants something from the listener.
- Test the interpretation with a brief scene. Give the character two plausible options with different costs, then let them choose and show a consequence. If the author wants to write the prose, give the scene beats and identify the choice they test. A minor character may need only a single exchange.

## Deepen an existing character

Start with a decision, relationship, or line that is already on the page. Find a place where the character's established habits could pull them in two directions. Develop that tension through a concrete action, omission, or exchange. Work within established facts and show which scene or relationship a proposed change would affect. If the author wants a different interpretation, revise the affected choices and dialogue in the existing version.

## Review a scene

Point to the exact action or phrase that creates the problem. Check what the character knows at that moment, what they want from the other person, and whether the scene gives the reader enough to follow their choice. Consider whether the apparent inconsistency could be deliberate and what would make it legible. Suggest the smallest useful change, such as setting up a motive earlier, moving a revelation, or changing a line. Leave the passage intact when reviewing. Rewrite the requested part when the author asks for a revision.

## Match the book

Use supplied prose to guide sentence rhythm, diction, distance from the viewpoint character, and what the narrator leaves implicit. Respect what the viewpoint allows the reader to know and when the genre expects a clue or revelation to become available. If the book's style is still open, use the tone the author described and treat the scene as a sketch. Let behavior, silence, and consequences carry characterization. Keep craft notes outside the scene and explain only the details that help the author make a decision.

## Keep accepted decisions

When the author accepts a version, carry those decisions forward in the conversation. If an established working notebook is available, record the accepted details there in its existing format and preserve other entries. If no notebook is available, give a concise recap the author can reuse and create a file only if requested. Keep open questions and alternate versions out of the accepted character notes. If a new direction conflicts with an earlier decision, identify the collision and follow the author's latest explicit choice.
